# Simplification Brief — Sponsor Network Instructions (Aug 10, 2026)

## The task
The participant-facing instructions in both surveys are correct but heavy (~1,700 words total).
Jose wants them SIMPLIFIED without changing the overarching message, incentive structure, or
design goals. Prolific participants skim; every extra sentence costs comprehension. The goal is
plain, short, skimmable text that a distracted reader still gets right.

## Hard constraints (a draft that violates any of these FAILS)
1. Every incentive fact must survive: all dollar amounts, the payout formulas, the max bonus,
   the random-selection payment rule. Participants must be able to compute their pay.
2. Every comprehension-check question must remain uniquely answerable from the instructions
   alone. The CQs are listed below; do not delete the facts they test.
3. Truthfulness: no new claims, no dropped disclosures. Everything told to participants must
   remain literally true (this includes the endorser-pay telegraph in the evaluator survey).
4. Design content is fixed: must-endorse (sponsor always endorses own-org candidate; chooses
   only strength); evaluators only ever see own-org endorsements; comparison = the other
   organization's candidate in the same set; three rounds; evaluators never see candidates'
   performance info; allocations pay the endorser win or lose.
5. Terminology: "wager", "$0.50 bank", "endorsement strength", "[ORGANIZATION 1: Vertex]" /
   "[ORGANIZATION 2: Beacon]" / "[ORGANIZATION 1: Atlas]" placeholders stay as-is.
   Never "stake" or "bet" as the noun for the wager DV. NO em dashes (use commas/periods).
6. Second person, active voice. No condescension, no filler ("please note", "it is important").
7. Bracketed [implementation notes] in the doc are NOT participant text: leave them out of your
   drafts entirely; they stay in the doc unchanged.

## Allowed (encouraged) simplification moves
- Cut repetition (ownership/affiliation is currently stated 4+ times in the Endorser tab; twice
  is enough: once at assignment, once at the decision screen).
- Bullets or short labeled lines for payout rules instead of paragraph prose.
- Front-load the action ("You will X"), background second; one idea per sentence.
- Just-in-time structure: rules that the SCREEN itself shows (e.g., the live payout preview
  under every slider) can be stated once briefly rather than re-explained.
- Merge rows' content where it reads better, but return text mapped to the SAME row structure
  (each row is one survey screen; do not invent or delete screens).
- Target reading level ~8th grade. Aim for roughly HALF the current word count per row (soft
  target; clarity beats hitting the number).

## Current participant-facing text

### ENDORSER survey (Stage 2, the sponsor)

[E-R4 | intro + task + slider | ~200 words]
In today's study you will act as an endorser. You have been assigned to represent [ORGANIZATION 1: Vertex].
In each candidate set, you will see two candidates: one from [ORGANIZATION 1: Vertex], the organization you represent, and one from [ORGANIZATION 2: Beacon], another organization. The candidate from [ORGANIZATION 1: Vertex] is your organization's candidate. You will also be shown both candidates' performance on a set of professional ability tests.
Your task is to decide how strongly to endorse your organization's candidate. You are not choosing between the candidates: in every set, your endorsement goes to the candidate from [ORGANIZATION 1: Vertex]. What you decide is how much to put behind that endorsement, based on how likely you think your candidate is to outperform the other organization's candidate on a separate logical reasoning test.
You will indicate your endorsement strength using a slider from 0 to 100. Higher values are a stronger endorsement: you are more confident in your candidate, and you are putting more behind them. Lower values are a weaker endorsement: you are less confident, and you are putting less at risk. Because you represent [ORGANIZATION 1: Vertex], evaluators will see your endorsement as a [ORGANIZATION 1: Vertex] endorser backing their own organization's candidate.

[E-R5 | who sees your endorsements | ~95 words]
Your endorsements will be reviewed by evaluators (participants in a separate study) and may be used to inform their decisions.
For each endorsement that is shown, the evaluator will see the organization you were assigned to represent, the candidate you endorsed (your organization's candidate), and your endorsement strength. Based on that information, the evaluator will decide what percentage of a $0.50 bank (0-100%) to wager on your endorsement being correct. At the end of their session, the evaluator will also decide how much future review to allocate to you and to your organization.

[E-R6 | earnings | ~250 words]
Your earnings have two parts. No matter what, you will receive $1 for completing this study. In addition, you can earn a bonus of up to $3. One of your endorsements will be selected at random, along with one evaluator who reviewed it, and your bonus will be based on that endorsement in three ways:
First, your candidate's performance. For the selected endorsement, you start with a bank of $0.50, and your endorsement strength works like a wager. If your candidate actually performs better on the logical reasoning test than the other organization's candidate, you earn $0.50 plus $0.50 times your strength percentage. If your candidate performs worse, you earn $0.50 minus $0.50 times your strength percentage. For example, at a strength of 80: you earn $0.90 if your candidate wins, and $0.10 if your candidate loses. A strong endorsement earns you more when you are right, and costs you more when you are wrong.
Second, the evaluator's trust. If your candidate wins, you also earn up to $1.00 depending on how much the evaluator wagered on your endorsement: $1.00 times their wager percentage. The more they trust your endorsement, the more you earn.
Third, the evaluator's allocations. At the end of their session, the evaluator decides how much future review to allocate to you and to your organization (0 to 10 slots each). Each slot allocated adds $0.05 to your bonus, up to $1.00 total. These allocations pay you regardless of whether your candidate won or lost.

[E-R7 | comprehension checks | KEEP STRUCTURE, light wording tweaks allowed]
1. Which scenario results in the largest bonus for you?
   (a) Your candidate loses at strength 100; the evaluator wagers 100%; the evaluator allocates 10 slots.
   (b) Your candidate wins at strength 0; the evaluator wagers 0%; the evaluator allocates 0 slots.
   (c) Your candidate wins at strength 80; the evaluator wagers 60%; the evaluator allocates 6 slots. (correct)
   (d) Your candidate wins at strength 20; the evaluator wagers 10%; the evaluator allocates 1 slot.
2. Which candidate do you endorse in each candidate set?
   (a) The candidate from [ORGANIZATION 1: Vertex], the organization I represent. (correct)
   (b) Whichever candidate I believe will perform better.
   (c) The candidate from [ORGANIZATION 2: Beacon].
   (d) I do not endorse anyone.

[E-R8 | who the candidates are | ~200 words]
Who are the candidates you will be endorsing?
These are participants who previously completed three professional assessment tasks: a logical reasoning test, a general knowledge quiz, and a word search puzzle. Each person was paid for correct answers, so everyone had a strong incentive to perform well. You will base your endorsement strength on the candidates' performance on the general knowledge quiz and word search puzzle tasks. Your endorsement strength represents how strongly you back your organization's candidate to outperform the other organization's candidate on the logical reasoning task.
Each candidate's profile will display their actual performance scores as percentile rankings on the general knowledge quiz and/or word search puzzle. For example, a candidate who scored at the 75th percentile performed better than 75 percent of all participants on that task. Some performance information may be listed as "Unknown," meaning you do not have data for that candidate on that particular task.
Each candidate's profile will also display the organization with which they are affiliated. In each candidate set, the candidate affiliated with [ORGANIZATION 1: Vertex] is your organization's candidate; the other candidate is affiliated with [ORGANIZATION 2: Beacon]. Candidates are shown by ID only, with no names or photos.

[E-R10 | objective reminder before decisions | ~130 words]
What is the objective when deciding your endorsement strength?
For each candidate set, use the slider to indicate how strongly you endorse your organization's candidate: how confident you are that they will outperform the other organization's candidate on the logical reasoning task, and how much you are willing to put behind them. A high strength is a strong public backing: it earns you more if your candidate wins, costs you more if they lose, and shows the evaluator you are putting your money where your mouth is. A low strength protects you when you are unsure, but earns less when your candidate wins and signals weaker backing.
Remember: you represent [ORGANIZATION 1: Vertex], and in every candidate set your endorsement goes to the [ORGANIZATION 1: Vertex] candidate. What you choose is how much to put behind them.

### EVALUATOR survey (Stage 3, the audience)

[V-R4 | fielded opening | 1 sentence]
In this study, you will review real endorsement decisions made by another participant. Your decisions are real: they determine your own bonus and can affect that participant's pay.

[V-R5 | framing intro | ~55 words, legacy text]
In this study, we are interested in understanding how people evaluate other people's decisions. Hence, we will be showing you information about another participant's real decisions and asking you how much you would be willing to wager part of your own compensation on their decisions.

[V-R6 | setup | ~230 words]
Before you decide how much you are willing to wager on someone else's decisions, you should first understand the set-up.
In a prior study, a group of participants completed three tasks: 1) a word-search puzzle, 2) a general-knowledge quiz, and 3) a logical-reasoning test. We will call these participants "candidates."
In a separate study, a different group of participants, whom we will call "endorsers," were each assigned to represent an organization. In each decision, an endorser saw two candidates: one from their own organization and one from another organization. The endorser's job was to endorse their own organization's candidate, and to decide how strongly to endorse them. Endorsers reviewed the candidates' performance on the knowledge quiz and word-search puzzle, and then used a slider to indicate how confident they were that their organization's candidate would score higher on the logical-reasoning test than the other organization's candidate in the same set.
Your task today, as an evaluator, is to decide how much you trust the judgments one endorser made. You will see a subset of one endorser's real endorsement decisions, not necessarily in the order they were made. For each endorsement, you will see the organization the endorser was assigned to represent, the endorsed candidate (always from the endorser's own organization), and the endorser's confidence. You will not see the candidates' performance information, only the endorser's decision. You will then have the opportunity to wager some of your own compensation on the accuracy of the endorser's decision.

[V-R7 | endorser incentives telegraph | ~140 words]
Endorsers had real incentives to make careful, well-calibrated endorsements. Their pay works like a wager: the stronger their endorsement, the more they earn if their candidate actually scores higher on the logical-reasoning test than the other organization's candidate, and the less they earn if their candidate scores lower.
Endorsers' pay also depends on you, in two ways. First, if their candidate wins, the more you wager on their endorsement, the larger their bonus. Second, at the end of today's session, you will decide how much future review to allocate to this endorser and to their organization; each slot you allocate adds to their bonus, whether their candidate won or lost.
(One endorsement, and one evaluator who reviewed it, will be selected at random for payment. So if your session is selected, your wagers and your allocations directly determine part of this endorser's pay.)

[V-R8 | wager mechanics + rounds | ~170 words]
Today you will evaluate three decisions made by the same endorser. Each decision involves a new, independent set of candidates. For each decision, you start with a bank of $0.50. You decide how much of it to wager on the endorser being correct.
If the endorser was right, then you get your $0.50 plus whatever you wagered.
If the endorser was wrong, then you get your $0.50 minus whatever you wagered.
For example, say you wager 10% (which is $0.05 of your $0.50):
Endorser right -> you earn $0.55
Endorser wrong -> you earn $0.45
Wagering 0% means you keep your $0.50 no matter what. Wagering 100% means you could earn $1.00 or $0.00.
An endorsement is correct if the endorsed candidate scores higher on the logical-reasoning test than the other organization's candidate in the same set. Your total bonus for these decisions is the sum of all three (up to $3.00 maximum). You will find out whether the endorser was right immediately after each wager.

4. How much do you start with for each decision? -> $0.50
